# Background for the reconstruction (`report/five-hands.html`)

Checked 19 November 2026. These are the period sources the story leans on beyond the manuscript
fact sheet (`fact-sheet.md`). None of them is about the manuscript. They are here so that the
staging uses things on record, and so that each can be re-read.

Tags as in the fact sheet: **OK** read at the source named; **SEC** secondary source or search
snippet only; **OURS** our own arithmetic or translation.

## 1. Baresch's letter to Kircher

Source: Philip Neal's transcription of APUG 557 f. 353rv,
http://philipneal.net/voynichsources/barschius_1637_latin/ (**OK**, read 19 Nov 2026). The page is
headed 1637; the letter is dated at its end "Pragae Anno Domini 1639. 27 die Aprilis".

| Latin | Our translation (**OURS**) | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| cumque in mea Bibliotheca Sphinx quaedam, Scriptura incognitorum characterum inutiliter occupasset locum | since a certain Sphinx, a writing in unknown characters, had been uselessly taking up space in my library | Scene 8 |
| Ex pictura herbarum, quarum plurimus est in Codice numerus, imaginum diversarum, Astrorum, aliarumque rerum, faciem chymicorum arcanorum referentium, conjicio totum esse medicinalem | From the pictures of herbs, of which there are a great many in the codex, of various images, of stars and of other things bearing the look of chemical secrets, I guess the whole to be medical | Scene 8 |
| quod causa plebeiorum occultandorum, tali industria vix author usus fuisset | the author would hardly have used such industry to conceal commonplace things | Scene 8, storyboard 17 |
| est valde probabile, aliquem virum bonum ... Regiones orientis adijsse, ibique thesauros Artis medicae Aegyptiacos ... talibus notis in libro eo defodisse | it is very probable that some good man ... went to the regions of the East and there ... buried the Egyptian treasures of the art of medicine in this book in such signs | not quoted; his own theory of origin |
| Augent probabilitatem herbae peregrinae, in Volumine depictae, notitiam hominum in partibus Germaniae subterfugientes | The foreign plants pictured in the volume, which escape the knowledge of people in German lands, add to the probability | not quoted |

He also says he had sent a copy of some of the writing a year and a half earlier through Father
Moretus, and that he studied medicine at the Sapienza in Rome in 1605.

## 2. The baths at Baden, 1416

Source: Poggio Bracciolini to Niccolò Niccoli, in Phyllis Gordan's translation (*Two Renaissance
Book Hunters*, 1974), as quoted and summarised at
https://qcurtius.com/2019/02/08/a-humanist-visits-the-german-baths/ (**SEC**: a summary with long
verbatim quotations; the full letter was not read. Project Gutenberg's copy of Shepherd's *Life of
Poggio*, which prints the letter, was unavailable on the day).

- Route from Constance: down the Rhine to Schaffhausen, on foot to Kaiserstuhl, then Baden. This
  is Baden in the Aargau. English Wikipedia's article on Poggio links it to Baden-Baden, which is
  wrong.
- About thirty baths, public and private; a fence between men and women in the public ones.
- Walkways above the pools where people stand and talk.
- "The men wear nothing but a leather apron, and the women put on linen shirts down to their
  knees, so cut on either side that they leave uncovered neck, bosom, arms, and shoulders."
- Meals in the water "with the tables floating".
- Girls playing instruments in the water; onlookers throwing coins; a ball "full of bells".
- Visitors, noble and common, from up to two hundred miles away; some bathe three or four times a
  day.
- Not found: any statement that barren women came for fertility. The story's patient is imagined.

The Council of Constance sat from 1414 to 1418; Poggio was there as a papal secretary.

## 3. Books of the kind being imitated

- **German house books of medical astrology.** The work known as the *Iatromathematisches
  Hausbuch* or *Deutscher Kalender* has 30 entries in the Handschriftencensus
  (https://handschriftencensus.de/werke, **SEC**: search listing). A study of the Strasbourg
  printer Hupfuff describes it as compiled "um 1400 im Nürnberger Dominikanerkloster" (**SEC**:
  search snippet from https://dokumen.pub/ein-verleger-sucht-sein-publikum-die-straburger-offizin-des-matthias-hupfuff-1497-98-1520-9783110937381-9783598249037.html).
  Contents named in snippets: calendar, prognostics, regimen, bloodletting, signs of the zodiac.
  A printed edition appeared at Ulm in 1498. We did not confirm a section on bathing and the page
  does not claim one.
- **Italian bath books.** The resemblance of the bathing pages to manuscripts of *De balneis
  Puteolanis* is recorded in the fact sheet (section 4).
- **Showpiece herbals.** Sergio Toresella's reading of the manuscript as a quack's showpiece, and
  his term "alchemical herbals", are reported by Zandbergen (fact sheet, section 4). His own paper
  was not read (**SEC**).

## 4. Paid writing outside the monasteries

- A workshop conventionally called the "Elsässische Werkstatt von 1418" was "active in Strasbourg
  between c. 1415 and 1425" (**SEC**: search snippet from a University of Antwerp repository
  paper, https://repository.uantwerpen.be/docstore/d:irua:31605). A paper manuscript of 256
  leaves from it is dated 1419 and signed by a scribe, Hans Coler (**SEC**: facsimile catalogue,
  https://www.omifacsimiles.com/cats/lengen_so.html). Diebold Lauber's workshop at Hagenau
  followed.
- Paper mill at Nuremberg, 1390 (Ulman Stromer): general knowledge, not re-checked.

These support two statements in Scene 1 and Scene 3 only: that lay scribes took paid work in the
region in these decades, and that their illustrated books were on paper.

## 5. Secret writing as display

Giovanni Fontana wrote two surviving illustrated manuscripts partly in cipher, the *Bellicorum
instrumentorum liber* (Munich, BSB Cod. icon. 242) and the *Secretum de thesauro experimentorum
ymaginationis hominum*, in northern Italy between about 1420 and 1440 (**SEC**: search snippets,
https://cryptiana.web.fc2.com/code/fontana.htm and https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=2459).
The page does not cite Fontana. He is kept here because he is the nearest dated parallel for
pictures with deliberately unreadable text, and because his text can be read once the cipher is
known, which this manuscript's cannot.

## 6. Things the story states from general knowledge

Not re-checked for this page: the craft of parchment making (lime, frame, crescent knife,
pumice); the behaviour of iron-gall ink (pale when written, darkening as it oxidises); pseudo-
script on painted textiles and painted books in European art of the thirteenth to fifteenth
centuries; the use of crosses and unintelligible words in medieval charms. A specialist should
look at each before a film states it.

## 7. A scribe's daily output

We looked for a citable rate and did not find a clean one. Michael Gullick's "How Fast Did Scribes
Write?" (1995) is the standard reference and was not read. One blog summary gives 0.28 leaves a
day for a part-time copyist. The story therefore states no duration for the writing. The text is
short: 4,130 lines.
