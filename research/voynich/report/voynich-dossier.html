The Voynich manuscript: what it most likely is, and how to check

A primer checked against the sources, the full text gathered and parsed, first tests run against texts of known origin, and a method for turning rival explanations into scenarios that can fail.

The setting is fairly firm

Calf parchment made between 1404 and 1438, ordinary iron-gall ink and period pigments, five scribes working as a team, and imagery that points to the Alpine arc between southern Germany and northern Italy. The book is laid out like a medical and astrological compendium: plants, a pharmacy of roots and jars, a zodiac calendar, bathing women, short paragraphs marked with stars.

The meaning is open, but narrower than it was

Nobody can read a word. In our tests the text's strangest habits (words that echo their neighbours, lines that behave as units) are reproduced by a copy-and-modify writing routine, and by neither natural languages nor the best published cipher model. That does not show the text is empty. It lists what any meaningful reading still has to explain.

The newest claim holds up in part

We rebuilt the 2026 page-order analysis from its description and got the same result, down to the same top-ranked sheet sequences. The finding that each folded sheet is a unit survives a scribe control the authors did not run. The proposed re-ordering of the sheets does no better than chance in our null test.

The way forward is a scoreboard

Write each explanation as a process that can be simulated. Fix the measurements in advance. Calibrate them on texts whose origin is known. Then check every text-based conclusion against physical evidence that the text analysis never saw.

Primer: the object and its history

The manuscript is a small parchment book, about 225 by 160 mm, kept at Yale's Beinecke Library as MS 408. 102 leaves survive of an original 116 or so, and several fold out into large sheets. Almost every page has drawings. Every page with writing uses the same script, which is found nowhere else. In the standard transliteration the running text comes to 39,026 words in 8,048 distinct forms, and 69% of those forms occur only once.

What the materials say

  • Date. Four leaves were radiocarbon dated at the University of Arizona in 2009: 1404 to 1438 with 95% probability. The result was announced but never published as a paper, and it dates the skins, not the writing.
  • Parchment. Calf, identified in 2014 by protein analysis at the University of York.
  • Ink and paint. McCrone Associates (2009) found iron-gall ink for both text and drawings, which were most likely made together, with azurite blue, a copper green and red ochre. Nothing is out of period. The inks of the folio numbers, the quire marks and the alphabet columns on the first page each differ from the text ink, so those are later additions.
  • Hands. Lisa Fagin Davis (2020) distinguishes five scribes. With two exceptions, each folded sheet is the work of a single scribe. Our page table from the transliteration agrees: 49 of 52 surviving sheets have one scribe.
  • Binding. Quire marks are fifteenth-century. Folio numbers came later (sixteenth or seventeenth century; the sources disagree). The present cover was added by the Roman Jesuits in the nineteenth century.

Where and by whom

No author, place or patron is recorded. The internal clues point in one broad direction without settling it.

  • The zodiac figures, including a crossbowman for Sagittarius, have their closest parallels in German-language manuscripts of the fifteenth century, especially from southern Germany.
  • The castle on the Rosettes foldout has swallowtail battlements, a feature found mostly in northern Italy.
  • Colour notes in the first scribe's hand are German (rot, red). The note on the last page contains German words. The month names on the zodiac pages are in a Romance dialect and a later hand; northern French, Occitan and Catalan have all been proposed.
  • Dress and hairstyles fit 1400 to 1430.

René Zandbergen, who maintains the standard reference site, puts it this way: the manuscript "was almost certainly created in the early 15th Century", and "at the very best the location where the MS was created remains speculation".

Emperor Rudolf II pays 600 florins to the Augsburg physician Carl Widemann for a small group of alchemical manuscripts (Guzy 2022). The sum matches the later story; the record does not name this book.

Jacobus Horčický de Tepenec, a chemist at Rudolf's court, signs the first page. The signature was later erased and shows under ultraviolet light. He is the first owner who can be positively identified, and he died in 1622.

Georg Baresch, a Prague alchemist, approaches the Jesuit scholar Athanasius Kircher about a book he cannot read. His letter of April 1639 is the earliest surviving description.
1665
Johannes Marcus Marci, who inherited the book from Baresch, sends it to Kircher on 19 August with a letter passing on hearsay, by then twenty years old, that Rudolf II paid 600 ducats for it and thought it the work of Roger Bacon.

Jesuit custody in Rome. In 1873 the book is hidden with others under labels marking the private library of the order's general, Petrus Beckx.

Kraus gives it to Yale.

One more development belongs here. A search of GitHub on the day this was written returned dozens of repositories from 2025 and 2026 announcing complete decipherments, as Sinhala, as a Latin and Occitan pharmacy book, as a midwives' manual, each with high claimed coverage and each excluding the others. Language models make it cheap to produce a fluent "translation" of anything. Readings are now abundant. What is scarce is a way to tell a right one from a wrong one, and that is the subject of the rest of this page.

Words at the edges of lines are different words. p begins 9.6% of line-first words and 0.5% of the rest. m ends 15.8% of line-last words and 0.9% of the rest. 85% of paragraphs open with one of four tall "gallows" glyphs, which begin 8% of words overall. Against a null that shuffles words within each line, the line-start effect has a z-score of 419. Language and cipher controls given the same line lengths show nothing (largest z-score 2.3). The generator shows the effect because its authors built it in.

Ordinary prose copied onto a page does not do this, because where a line breaks is an accident of word widths. Whatever produced the text worked line by line. One caution: our controls had their line breaks imposed, so they are null by construction. Transcriptions of real fifteenth-century books that keep the original line breaks would be the fairer comparison.

There is also a weak pull from the line above. Using the pixel positions of words, a word is 11% more likely to match a word sitting within a word-width and a half of it in the line above than a word further along that line (4.85% against 4.35%, z = 3.1). Counting by word position instead, which lets us run the controls too, the manuscript shows 8% (z = 3.0), the language and cipher controls show nothing, and the generator shows up to 19%. The effect is small, and it points the same way as the echo between neighbours: words resemble words already written nearby.

This is our check on the 2026 paper by Layfield and Davis. They measured how alike pages are with latent semantic analysis and found that the two inner pages of a sheet are as alike as the two sides of a leaf, while facing pages on different sheets are less alike. In two Latin manuscripts with ordinary nested quires the pattern is the reverse. They conclude each sheet was written to be read on its own.

The result replicates. We wrote the pipeline again from the paper's description, on a newer version of the transliteration. Our averages are 0.419, 0.297 and 0.468 for the two sides of a leaf, facing pages and inner pairs; theirs are 0.383, 0.298 and 0.433. Searching all orders of the sheets in quires 13 and 20, we get the same top-ranked sequence as they do in both.

It survives a control they did not run. In the herbal quires, sheets by different scribes are interleaved, so 16 of the 47 facing pairs there are by two different scribes, and those pairs score 0.08 against 0.31 for same-scribe pairs. That alone would make facing pages look unrelated. So we compared each pair with other pages by the same scribe in the same quire. The inner pair of a sheet is still +0.155 more alike (+0.103 to +0.205), in the herbal quires (+0.15), quire 13 (+0.16) and quire 20 (+0.19) alike. Facing pages on different sheets are exactly at baseline (-0.001).

The proposed new order is not yet supported. In our replication the best of the 120 possible orders of the sheets in quire 13 scores 0.572, against 0.478 as bound (they report 0.517 against 0.461). But a search always finds something. When we scramble the table of sheet-to-sheet scores and run the same search, it finds an order at least that good 56% of the time in quire 13 and 29% of the time in quire 20. The text alone cannot yet say which sheet followed which. The authors' planned comparison of water stains across sheets is the right next test, because it is independent of the text.

This establishes a fact about production. The book was written sheet by sheet, each sheet folded and filled in page order. It does not by itself show the pages carry a continuous meaning, since a scribe varying words already on the sheet would produce the same gradient.

Currier's languages A and B differ sharply: the sequence ed appears in 20.6% of B words and 0.3% of A words. Holding the subject fixed (herbal pages) and moving from scribe 1 to another scribe keeps 34–41% of the similarity. Holding the scribe fixed and changing the subject keeps 56–64%. Scribes 2 and 3, who both write B, are nearly interchangeable (90%). So the dialect is mostly a property of who was writing. Subject matters too, but less.

Labels and opening words do not yet behave like names

If the one-word labels beside drawings are names, and the text on the page discusses the things drawn, a page's labels should turn up in its own text. They barely do: 6.7% of a page's labels appear in a sample of its own text, against 5.6% in the text of a sister page by the same scribe (difference +1.1 points, 95% interval -0.4 to +2.8, 33 pages). Labels do have a shape of their own: 58% begin with o, against 22% of running words.

The first word of a paragraph is unique in the whole book 49% of the time, against 11% for words in mid-line, which has encouraged the idea that herbal pages open with the plant's name. But 71% of opening words become an ordinary attested word when their first glyph is removed (58% for mid-line words, 81% for other line-first words). Much of their uniqueness is an added glyph.

Two limits on what this shows. The cipher column is one cipher, built as a proof of concept; a different design might reproduce more. And every meaningful control is continuous prose. Five tableaus

A tableau here is a complete scene: who made the book, why, by what procedure, and what the text therefore is. All five share the same setting, because the evidence for the setting does not depend on reading the text. They differ in what the writing carries. The weights are our working judgement after the tests above. They are soft, they are not a consensus, and the method in the next section exists to replace them with scores.

30–40%working weight

A book made to look learned

The text never encoded anything. A group produced an impressive illustrated book for a buyer, a patron or their own practice, and filled it with writing-like text by a simple routine: write a word, vary one already on the sheet, keep to the conventions of lines and paragraphs. The drawings imitate the kinds of book that carried authority.

Explains every local habit we measured, the scribe-bound dialects, labels that are not tied to their text, and a century without a reading.

Has to explain why five trained scribes sustained it for over two hundred pages, why vocabulary still shifts with subject within one scribe, and the passage-to-passage coherence that Layfield and Davis report as unlike the generator's.

Would rise if a copy-and-modify model predicts held-out pages better than any cipher-plus-language model, scribal corrections turn out to be nearly absent, and no link between text and picture survives once the order of production is controlled.

25–35%working weight

A working compendium in cipher

A small group with medical interests compiles herbal, pharmaceutical, astrological and bathing lore from ordinary sources and writes it in a devised script under a systematic, reversible encoding, for secrecy or for standing. Each scribe encodes sheet by sheet with personal habits or tables.

Explains the genre layout, the team, and the subject effect within a scribe. It fits the period: secret writing was urged for exactly this material, and substitution ciphers with several symbols per letter were in use in north Italian chanceries by mid-century.

Has to explain the echo between neighbouring words, the repeats, the line edges and the pull from the line above, none of which the Naibbe cipher produces; dialects that follow the scribe; and why a hand cipher of the 1420s has resisted every attack.

Would rise if a cipher applied to real period text reproduces the whole table above, a mechanical decoding passes the gate below, or labels map to picture-appropriate words more often than shuffled labels do.

20–30%working weight

A compendium that lost its meaning in the making

The makers worked from real sources and meant to record them, but the procedure cannot be run backwards: abbreviation past the point of ambiguity, a private memory notation, arbitrary padding, or copying by pattern in which each scribe rebuilt words from stock parts. The book is organised like its sources. The text no longer carries them. Zandbergen names this possibility: meaning "lost in the process of its creation".

Explains at once the genre layout and the statistical likeness to generated text, the scribe-bound habits, the failure of decipherment and the untied labels.

Has to explain what residue of the sources survives, and how to tell it from the first tableau at all.

Would rise if rare words track pictures across distant pages (the same plant drawn twice, the same words) without any consistent decoding, or if errors typical of copying from an exemplar sit alongside generator-like statistics.

5–10%working weight

An invented or revealed language

A constructed vocabulary in which words are assembled from classifying prefixes and suffixes, as Friedman and Tiltman suspected, or visionary writing in the manner of Hildegard of Bingen's twelfth-century Lingua Ignota.

Explains the rigid word structure and predictable glyphs. Has to explain five scribes sharing it in two dialects, and why word families are not tied to subjects or labels. Systematic languages of this kind are otherwise unknown before the seventeenth century.

under 5%working weight

An unknown language, plainly written

A spoken language set down in an invented alphabet with no concealment.

Explains the word-frequency curve and the section vocabulary. Has to explain glyphs far more predictable than in any script we tested, neighbouring words that attract where in every language they repel, lines that act as units, and two dialects for one subject split by scribe.

What all five agree on

This is the part of the answer that does not wait on the text. The book was made in the first decades of the fifteenth century, most likely within a generation of the parchment. It was made by a small team, not a lone author, working one folded sheet at a time, drawing first and writing after. Its makers knew the illustrated medical and astrological books of their region and built this one on their plan. On Davis's evidence it was bound out of order within the century. It was in Prague by the early seventeenth century, and it has been unread for as long as there are records of anyone trying. Davis's own suggestion is "a book of women's folk knowledge that's being preserved", with the caution that "you cannot prove that yet".

How to validate a tableau

The field has plenty of ideas and no agreed way for one of them to lose. These steps make each tableau a claim that the evidence can score. They are in order, because the later ones depend on the earlier.

  1. Write each tableau as a process you can run

    A scenario becomes testable when it is a procedure that takes inputs (a source text or none, a set of rules, five scribes with their habits, sheets written in page order) and emits pages. The copy-and-modify generator and the Naibbe cipher are the first two such simulators. The meaning-lost tableau needs one. A tableau that cannot be simulated cannot be scored.

  2. Fix the measurements before looking

    Agree on a panel of fingerprints in advance: the six rows of the table above, word structure, the sheet gradient, section and scribe effects. Set aside a share of the sheets that nobody examines until the end. Without this, every analyst tunes until something appears, and the text is small enough (39,026 words) to be overfitted by hand.

  3. Calibrate every measurement on texts of known origin

    Before a statistic is trusted on the manuscript it must separate known cases: real languages in period subjects, period ciphers over real text, generated text, and gibberish written by people. Add the classes still missing, above all lists, recipe collections, calendars and tables, and transcriptions that keep original line breaks. Require the result to hold across transliterations.

  4. Score the tableaus by prediction

    Fit each simulator on most of the sheets and ask how well it predicts the words on the held-out ones, in bits per word. Compare a copy model (the next word is a variant of one nearby), a cipher over a language model, and mixtures. This gives a likelihood ratio between tableaus in place of an argument, and it shows how much of the text each one leaves unexplained.

  5. Control for who, what and when

    Scribe, subject and order of writing are tangled together. Compare like with like, as in the same-scribe baseline above. For any result found by searching, such as a best page order or a best key, run the same search on scrambled data to learn what it finds by chance.

  6. Check text against evidence the text never touched

    The strongest validation is agreement between independent kinds of evidence. Physical: the water stain, paint offsets, sewing holes, which sheets were cut from the same skin, which were written with the same batch of ink. Visual: drawings matched to dated traditions by people who have not seen the text results. If the sheet order from stains agrees with the order from text similarity, both gain. If rare words follow a plant from the herbal pages to the pharmacy pages once scribe and order are controlled, the text refers to something.

  7. Hold every claimed reading to one gate

    See the box below. The same gate applies to a scholar's proposal and to a language model's.

  8. Qualify methods on a blind challenge set

    Build a public set of documents made by known processes, some enciphered, some generated, some real languages in invented scripts, all in the same transliteration style, with the answers sealed. A method earns the right to pronounce on the manuscript by classifying these correctly first.

The gate for a claimed decipherment

  1. The mapping from script to language is written down in full and can be applied by someone else without judgement calls.
  2. It has fewer free choices than the text has information. Count the choices; a key with thousands of entries for 8,048 word forms explains nothing.
  3. It was fixed before the held-out sheets were opened, and it yields grammatical text there.
  4. Applied to scrambled manuscript text and to generated text, it fails. A method that "reads" gibberish reads nothing.
  5. Run forward on real period text, it reproduces the fingerprints: predictable glyphs, echoing neighbours, line edges, two dialects.
  6. Labels come out as words that suit their pictures more often than labels shuffled between pictures do.
  7. Two people using the key independently produce the same reading of the same page.

What to do next

In order of value for effort. The first group needs only the material already gathered.

At the desk

  1. Add the missing controls. Recipe collections, calendars, tables with Roman numerals, and line-by-line transcriptions of real fifteenth-century books. If lists and numerals echo their neighbours the way the manuscript does, the cipher tableau gains.
  2. Fit and score the simulators. A copy model, a cipher over a language model and their mixtures, compared on held-out sheets.
  3. Count the corrections. Go through the page images for erasures, overwriting and insertions. Copying a real text from an exemplar leaves characteristic slips; inventing text leaves few.
  4. Test the repeated plants. Match pharmacy drawings to herbal drawings by eye, blind to the text, then ask whether matched pages share rare words more than unmatched pages by the same scribe.
  5. Repeat every result on a second transliteration.

With the library

  1. Map the water stain, offsets and sewing holes across all sheets to reconstruct their order without the text.
  2. Image the whole book multispectrally.
  3. Sample parchment and ink sheet by sheet to recover skins and ink batches.

With the community

  1. Publish the fingerprint panel and the challenge set, and invite both sides to register predictions before the held-out sheets are opened.
