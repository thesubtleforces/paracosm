The Voynich manuscript: what it most likely is, and how to check

A primer checked against the sources, the full text gathered and parsed, tests run against texts of known origin, a first validation round that overturned some of our own readings, and a method for turning rival explanations into scenarios that can fail.

The setting is fairly firm

Calf parchment made between 1404 and 1438, ordinary iron-gall ink and period pigments, five scribes working as a team, and imagery that points to the Alpine arc between southern Germany and northern Italy. The book is laid out like a medical and astrological compendium: plants, a pharmacy of roots and jars, a zodiac calendar, bathing women, short paragraphs marked with stars.

A written word here does not stand for a word

On sheets held back from training, knowing the previous word tells you almost nothing about the next: {{v_gram_voy_med}} bits, against at least {{v_gram_min}} in every one of {{v_n_meaningful}} meaningful texts we scored. That closes every word-for-word reading. What remains is a cipher that spends a whole written word on a letter or two, or text that carries no message.

Neither published model fits yet

The best cipher model has none of the manuscript's local habits. The best meaningless-text model has {{v_rep_gen_x}} times too much of them. And three habits we first read as signs of a copying routine turn up in real verse, lists and abbreviated manuscripts, so we have withdrawn them.

The leading tableau keeps a narrow lead

A book made to look learned, with no message in its text, stays in front at 35 to 40%. A real compendium whose meaning did not survive its own encoding is second. A true cipher is third, and it would have to be a kind more elaborate than any known from those decades.

The newest claim holds up in part

We rebuilt the 2026 page-order analysis from its description and got the same result on three transliterations, down to the same top-ranked sheet sequences. Sheets as units survives a scribe control the authors did not run. The proposed re-ordering does no better than chance in our null test.

How the book was made is becoming answerable

Sheet by sheet, in page order, by a team, drawing first. Whether it says anything is a separate question, and it may have no answer. The value of this exercise is in showing how one would know.

Primer: the object and its history

The manuscript is a small parchment book, about 225 by 160 mm, kept at Yale's Beinecke Library as MS 408. {{n_folios}} leaves survive of an original 116 or so, and several fold out into large sheets. Almost every page has drawings. Every page with writing uses the same script, which is found nowhere else. In the standard transliteration the running text comes to {{n_tokens}} words in {{n_types}} distinct forms, and {{hapax_pct}} of those forms occur only once.

What the materials say

  • Date. Four leaves were radiocarbon dated at the University of Arizona in 2009: 1404 to 1438 with 95% probability. The result was announced but never published as a paper, and it dates the skins, not the writing.
  • Parchment. Calf, identified in 2014 by protein analysis at the University of York.
  • Ink and paint. McCrone Associates (2009) found iron-gall ink for both text and drawings, which were most likely made together, with azurite blue, a copper green and red ochre. Nothing is out of period. The inks of the folio numbers, the quire marks and the alphabet columns on the first page each differ from the text ink, so those are later additions.
  • Hands. Lisa Fagin Davis (2020) distinguishes five scribes. With two exceptions, each folded sheet is the work of a single scribe. Our page table from the transliteration agrees: 49 of 52 surviving sheets have one scribe.
  • Binding. Quire marks are fifteenth-century. Folio numbers came later (sixteenth or seventeenth century; the sources disagree). The present cover was added by the Roman Jesuits in the nineteenth century.

Where and by whom

No author, place or patron is recorded. The internal clues point in one broad direction without settling it.

  • The zodiac figures, including a crossbowman for Sagittarius, have their closest parallels in German-language manuscripts of the fifteenth century, especially from southern Germany.
  • The castle on the Rosettes foldout has swallowtail battlements, a feature found mostly in northern Italy.
  • Colour notes in the first scribe's hand are German (rot, red). The note on the last page contains German words. The month names on the zodiac pages are in a Romance dialect and a later hand; northern French, Occitan and Catalan have all been proposed.
  • Dress and hairstyles fit 1400 to 1430.

René Zandbergen, who maintains the standard reference site, puts it this way: the manuscript "was almost certainly created in the early 15th Century", and "at the very best the location where the MS was created remains speculation".

Who owned it

Quire marks are added and the book is sewn between wooden boards. On Davis's reading this first binding already put the sheets in the wrong order.

Emperor Rudolf II pays 600 florins to the Augsburg physician Carl Widemann for a small group of alchemical manuscripts (Guzy 2022). The sum matches the later story; the record does not name this book.

Jacobus Horčický de Tepenec, a chemist at Rudolf's court, signs the first page. The signature was later erased and shows under ultraviolet light. He is the first owner who can be positively identified, and he died in 1622.

Georg Baresch, a Prague alchemist, approaches the Jesuit scholar Athanasius Kircher about a book he cannot read. His letter of April 1639 is the earliest surviving description.

Johannes Marcus Marci, who inherited the book from Baresch, sends it to Kircher on 19 August with a letter passing on hearsay, by then twenty years old, that Rudolf II paid 600 ducats for it and thought it the work of Roger Bacon.

Jesuit custody in Rome. In 1873 the book is hidden with others under labels marking the private library of the order's general, Petrus Beckx.

1961
After the deaths of Voynich (1930) and his widow Ethel (1960), Anne Nill sells it to the dealer H. P. Kraus for $24,500. Kraus asks $100,000, then $160,000, and finds no buyer.
1969
Kraus gives it to Yale.

One more development belongs here. A search of GitHub on the day this was written returned dozens of repositories from 2025 and 2026 announcing complete decipherments, as Sinhala, as a Latin and Occitan pharmacy book, as a midwives' manual, each with high claimed coverage and each excluding the others. Language models make it cheap to produce a fluent "translation" of anything. Readings are now abundant. What is scarce is a way to tell a right one from a wrong one, and that is the subject of the rest of this page.

First tests, with controls

Round 1, as first run. The validation round that follows withdrew or weakened three readings made in this section: that line effects, likeness to the word above, and exact repeats point to a copying routine. They are left here as written so the record shows what changed.

Each test asks one question of the manuscript and puts the same question to the control texts. The running text is cut into equal-sized samples so that numbers are comparable. Words are quoted in EVA, the standard transliteration alphabet; its letters are labels for shapes, not sounds.

The next glyph is far easier to guess than in any language

How many bits of surprise each glyph carries once you know the one before it. One dot per text. The manuscript sits with the cipher and the generator, far from every language.

{{chart_h2}}

The manuscript scores {{h2_voy}} bits against {{h2_nat}} for the {{n_nat}} language samples. The gap survives a change of alphabet: {{h2_merged}} bits when EVA's strokes are merged into whole glyphs, {{h2_currier}} in Currier's alphabet, {{h2_v101}} in v101. Naibbe ciphertext ({{h2_cip}}) and the generator ({{h2_gen}}) both match. So this famous property rules out ordinary writing of any language we tried, including an unvowelled and an abbreviated one, and says nothing about cipher against meaningless text.

Words echo the words just before them

How much more likely two words are to differ by exactly one glyph (as qokedy and qokeedy do) than they would be if the words of the page were shuffled. Thin vertical lines show the spread across texts in each group.

{{chart_lag}}

A word in the manuscript is {{near1_voy_pct}} more likely than chance to be a one-glyph variant of the word before it. In all {{n_nat_lag}} language controls and both cipher texts the opposite holds: neighbouring words are less alike than chance ({{near1_nat}} and {{near1_cip}} times), because grammar alternates kinds of word. The generator behaves like the manuscript ({{near1_gen}}). Exact repeats show the same split. They make up {{rep_voy}} of adjacent pairs in the manuscript. That is {{rep_voy_x}} times the rate in a shuffled sample, and still {{exact1_voy}} times the rate when only the words within each page are shuffled. Every language and cipher sample falls well below both.

The line is a unit

Share of words that begin (left) or end (right) with a given glyph, for the word at the edge of a line and for all other words.

{{chart_edges}}

Words at the edges of lines are different words. p begins {{edge_p_at}} of line-first words and {{edge_p_else}} of the rest. m ends {{edge_m_at}} of line-last words and {{edge_m_else}} of the rest. {{gallows_open}} of paragraphs open with one of four tall "gallows" glyphs, which begin {{gallows_all}} of words overall. Against a null that shuffles words within each line, the line-start effect has a z-score of {{line_z_first}}. Language and cipher controls given the same line lengths show nothing (largest z-score {{line_nat_maxz}}). The generator shows the effect because its authors built it in.

Ordinary prose copied onto a page does not do this, because where a line breaks is an accident of word widths. Whatever produced the text worked line by line. One caution: our controls had their line breaks imposed, so they are null by construction. Transcriptions of real fifteenth-century books that keep the original line breaks would be the fairer comparison.

There is also a weak pull from the line above. Using the measured positions of words, a word is {{geo_pct}} more likely to match a word sitting within a word-width and a half of it in the line above than a word further along that line ({{geo_near}} against {{geo_far}}, z = {{geo_z}}). Counting by word position instead, which lets us run the controls too, the manuscript shows {{vert_voy}} (z = {{vert_voy_z}}), the language and cipher controls show nothing, and the generator shows up to {{vert_gen_max_pct}}. The effect is small, and it points the same way as the echo between neighbours: words resemble words already written nearby.

Each folded sheet is a unit, read 1r, 1v, 2r, 2v

A sheet folded once gives four pages. The numbers are how much more alike two pages are than two pages by the same scribe elsewhere in the quire. Similarity falls with distance in the order a folded sheet would be read, and vanishes between sheets.

{{diagram_sheet}}
{{chart_pairs}}

This is our check on the 2026 paper by Layfield and Davis. They measured how alike pages are with latent semantic analysis and found that the two inner pages of a sheet are as alike as the two sides of a leaf, while facing pages on different sheets are less alike. In two Latin manuscripts with ordinary nested quires the pattern is the reverse. They conclude each sheet was written to be read on its own.

The result replicates. We wrote the pipeline again from the paper's description, on a newer version of the transliteration. Our averages are {{lsa_conf}}, {{lsa_face}} and {{lsa_conj}} for the two sides of a leaf, facing pages and inner pairs; theirs are 0.383, 0.298 and 0.433. Searching all orders of the sheets in quires 13 and 20, we get the same top-ranked sequence as they do in both.

It survives a control they did not run. In the herbal quires, sheets by different scribes are interleaved, so {{face_h_diff_n}} of the {{face_h_n}} facing pairs there are by two different scribes, and those pairs score {{face_h_diff}} against {{face_h_same}} for same-scribe pairs. That alone would make facing pages look unrelated. So we compared each pair with other pages by the same scribe in the same quire. The inner pair of a sheet is still {{ex_conj}} more alike ({{ex_conj_ci}}), in the herbal quires ({{ex_conj_h}}), quire 13 ({{ex_conj_q13}}) and quire 20 ({{ex_conj_q20}}) alike. Facing pages on different sheets are exactly at baseline ({{ex_face}}).

The proposed new order is not yet supported. In our replication the best of the 120 possible orders of the sheets in quire 13 scores {{ro13_best}}, against {{ro13_bound}} as bound (they report 0.517 against 0.461). But a search always finds something. When we scramble the table of sheet-to-sheet scores and run the same search, it finds an order at least that good {{ro13_pct}} of the time in quire 13 and {{ro20_pct}} of the time in quire 20. The text alone cannot yet say which sheet followed which. The authors' planned comparison of water stains across sheets is the right next test, because it is independent of the text.

This establishes a fact about production. The book was written sheet by sheet, each sheet folded and filled in page order. It does not by itself show the pages carry a continuous meaning, since a scribe varying words already on the sheet would produce the same gradient.

The two "languages" follow the scribe more than the subject

How much vocabulary similarity survives when one thing changes. 100% would mean the two groups of pages are as alike as pages within each group.

{{chart_scribe_subject}}

Currier's languages A and B differ sharply: the sequence ed appears in {{ed_B}} of B words and {{ed_A}} of A words. Holding the subject fixed (herbal pages) and moving from scribe 1 to another scribe keeps {{scribe_keep}} of the similarity. Holding the scribe fixed and changing the subject keeps {{subject_keep}}. Scribes 2 and 3, who both write B, are nearly interchangeable ({{s2s3_keep}}). So the dialect is mostly a property of who was writing. Subject matters too, but less.

Labels and opening words do not yet behave like names

If the one-word labels beside drawings are names, and the text on the page discusses the things drawn, a page's labels should turn up in its own text. They barely do: {{lab_own}} of a page's labels appear in a sample of its own text, against {{lab_other}} in the text of a sister page by the same scribe (difference {{lab_diff}} points, 95% interval {{lab_ci}}, {{lab_pages}} pages). Labels do have a shape of their own: {{lab_o}} begin with o, against {{run_o}} of running words.

The first word of a paragraph is unique in the whole book {{open_unique}} of the time, against {{mid_unique}} for words in mid-line, which has encouraged the idea that herbal pages open with the plant's name. But {{open_known}} of opening words become an ordinary attested word when their first glyph is removed ({{mid_known}} for mid-line words, {{linefirst_known}} for other line-first words). Much of their uniqueness is an added glyph.

Which measurements can tell the candidates apart

This table is the point of running controls. A row is useful only where the control columns differ. The first row cannot separate a cipher from meaningless text. The others can, and on each of them the manuscript sits with the generator or beyond it.

Two limits on what this shows. The cipher column is one cipher, built as a proof of concept; a different design might reproduce more. And every meaningful control is continuous prose. Validation round 1

The first round ended with five checks that needed only material already to hand. All five have now been run. Two of them changed our reading.

The controls that were missing

Round 1 compared the manuscript with continuous prose, where line breaks fall by accident. Three kinds of meaningful text were missing: verse, where the line is the unit by design; lists and recipes, which repeat themselves; and real manuscripts transcribed line by line with their abbreviations. We added six Latin verse texts (among them a calendar poem, a verse herbal and a verse list of saints' days), four list and recipe texts, and 21 Latin manuscripts of the eleventh to fifteenth centuries, including medical recipe collections and a prayer book.

{{table_newcontrols}}
  • Line endings are ordinary. At equal numbers of lines, the manuscript's line-end effect is {{v1_end_ratio}} times that of verse and lists. Text composed in lines ends its lines in particular ways.
  • Line starts are not. The line-start effect is {{v1_start_ratio}} times larger than in any control of the same size. It is the same in kind as in verse and lists, and far stronger.
  • Likeness to the word above is commoner in real text. Verse scores {{v1_above_verse}} and lists {{v1_above_list}}, against 1.08 for the manuscript. Parallel phrasing produces it. It is no sign of copying.
  • Real formulaic text echoes too. In Cato's farm recipes a word is {{v1_cato_near}} times as likely as chance to be one letter off its neighbour, and {{v1_ms_near_over1}} of {{v1_ms_n}} abbreviated manuscripts exceed chance (up to {{v1_ms_near_max}}). Verse never does ({{v1_verse_near}}).
  • Predictable glyphs remain unique. Every new text scores between {{v1_h2_min}} and {{v1_h2_max}} bits. Heavy abbreviation raises the figure.

So three of the four habits that Round 1 read as signs of a copying routine are also habits of real texts written in lines, in formulas or in abbreviation. We withdraw them as evidence against meaning.

Word order carries almost no information

Every word on a held-out sheet is predicted by a mixture of simple predictors. The panels show how much each one helps. Grammar helps every meaningful text and does nothing for the manuscript, the cipher or the generator.

{{chart_gains}}

This is step 4 of the method below, run for the first time. Five folds of sheets are held out in turn. A unigram model is trained on the rest, and three predictors are added one at a time: the previous word (grammar), an exact copy of a word among the last forty on the page (repeat), and a one-glyph variation of such a word (vary).

Grammar. The manuscript gains {{v_gram_voy}} bits per word across six samples. The lowest of {{v_n_meaningful}} meaningful texts is Virgil's Georgics at {{v_gram_min}}, and typical prose gains {{v_gram_prose_med}}. The Naibbe cipher ({{v_gram_cip}}) and the generator ({{v_gram_gen}}) sit with the manuscript. This excludes every reading in which a written word stands for a word: a plain language, a word-for-word code, an invented language with a syntax. A cipher survives only if it works below the word, as Naibbe does, spending a written word on one or two letters. On that reading the whole book would hold about ten thousand words of plain text, and a one-word label would hold a letter or two, which cannot be a name.

Repeat and vary. The manuscript gains {{v_rep_voy}} and {{v_vary_voy}} bits. The cipher gains nothing on either. The generator gains {{v_rep_gen}} and {{v_vary_gen}}: {{v_rep_gen_x}} times too much repetition. Neither published simulator matches. Any future model of either kind now has three numbers to hit.

The predictors are deliberately crude, and a rule that sorts texts by these three numbers recovers the class of only about half our controls, so we do not use it to classify the manuscript. The grammar result does not depend on it.

Three transliterations

{{table_translit}}

The headline results hold on Takahashi's reading and on Claston's, which uses a different alphabet with about three times as many glyph types. One does not: exact repetition of the neighbouring word is {{v3_exact}} times chance in the three files, so in Claston's finer alphabet it sits at chance. We no longer count it.

Corrections, and the same plant twice

The transcribers flagged {{v4_total}} possible corrections, erasures or glyphs written above the line in {{n_tokens}} words, one per {{v4_words_per}} words, on {{v4_pages}} pages. That is a lower bound from notes made in passing, and we have no matched rate from ordinary copying to set beside it. A census from the photographs is still to do.

The 1998 interlinear file records that the plant on f39r is drawn again on f95r2, by scribes {{v5_scribes}}. If the text described the plant, the two pages should share wording. Their similarity sits at the {{v5_pct_lsa}}th percentile of {{v5_ref_n}} comparable page pairs, and at the {{v5_pct_tfidf}}th by a second measure. One pair proves little. Under a cipher with several symbols for each letter the same sentence would come out differently every time, so this test can speak only against word-level naming.

Five tableaus

A tableau here is a complete scene: who made the book, why, by what procedure, and what the text therefore is. All five share the same setting, because the evidence for the setting does not depend on reading the text. They differ in what the writing carries. The weights are our working judgement after the validation round. They are soft and they are not a consensus. The order did not change; the gaps did, and the cipher tableau is now much narrower in what it can be.

A book made to look learned

The text never encoded anything. A group produced an impressive illustrated book for a patron or their own practice, and filled it with writing-like text by a simple routine: build a word from a few stock parts, vary one already on the sheet, open a paragraph with a tall glyph, start and end lines in set ways. The drawings imitate the kinds of book that carried authority.

Explains the predictable glyphs, the absence of grammar, the echo, the heavily marked line starts, dialects that follow the scribe, labels untied to their text, and a century without a reading.

Has to explain why five trained scribes sustained it for over two hundred pages, why vocabulary shifts with subject within one scribe, and why its best published simulator repeats itself {{v_rep_gen_x}} times more than the manuscript does.

Would rise if a generator with weaker locality hits all three prediction numbers, a census finds scribal corrections nearly absent, and no link between text and picture survives a larger test.

A compendium that lost its meaning in the making

The makers worked from real sources and meant to record them, but the procedure cannot be run backwards: a private memory notation, arbitrary padding, or copying by pattern in which each scribe rebuilt words from stock parts. The book is organised like its sources. The text no longer carries them. Zandbergen names this possibility: meaning "lost in the process of its creation".

Explains everything the first tableau explains, and the fidelity of the layout to real medical books.

A working compendium in a letter-level cipher

A small group with medical interests writes real herbal, astrological and bathing lore under a reversible cipher that works below the word, each written word standing for a letter or two, with several alternatives for each. Secret writing was urged for exactly this material, and ciphers with more than one symbol per letter were in use in north Italian chanceries by mid-century.

Explains the predictable glyphs and the absence of grammar, as Naibbe shows, and gives five people a reason to do the work.

Has to explain the echo and the marked line starts, which Naibbe lacks entirely; vocabulary that shifts with scribe and with section, which a letter cipher cannot get from its content; and a design more elaborate than any cipher documented from those decades. It also makes the book short, about ten thousand words, and its labels a letter or two long.

Would rise if a cipher run on real period text reproduces the three prediction numbers and the line starts, or a mechanical decoding passes the gate below.

What all five agree on

This is the part of the answer that does not wait on the text. The book was made in the first decades of the fifteenth century, most likely within a generation of the parchment. It was made by a small team, not a lone author, working one folded sheet at a time, drawing first and writing after. Its makers knew the illustrated medical and astrological books of their region and built this one on their plan. On Davis's evidence it was bound out of order within the century. It was in Prague by the early seventeenth century, and it has been unread for as long as there are records of anyone trying. Davis's own suggestion is "a book of women's folk knowledge that's being preserved", with the caution that "you cannot prove that yet".

The leading tableau is told in full in Five Hands: nine scenes, each claim marked as documented, measured, inferred or imagined, with a list of findings that would overturn it and the groundwork for a film.

What this exercise adds, and what it takes away

A fair account has to say both. Most of what is here confirms what specialists already knew or suspected. Some of it is new. Some of it removes things, including things we said ourselves a day earlier.

What it adds

  1. An independent replication of the 2026 page-order result, built from the paper's text alone, on three transliterations, reaching the same top-ranked sheet sequences.
  2. A control and a null test for that result. The claim that each sheet is a unit survives the scribe control. The proposed re-ordering does not survive the null. Both tell the authors which half to build on, and both agree with their own plan to test the order on physical evidence.
  3. A new observation about production. Within a sheet, likeness falls in page order: inner pair, then the two sides of a leaf, then the diagonals, then the outer pair. A folded sheet filled from first page to last predicts exactly that.
  4. One table on one scale. A cipher, a generator and more than forty real texts take the same tests. The field has many statistics and few calibrations.
  5. Controls nobody had set beside the manuscript. Real manuscripts transcribed line by line, verse and lists overturned three of our own first-round readings within a day.
  6. A sharp negative. A written word does not stand for a word. That closes at once the large family of word-for-word readings, to which most announced translations belong.
  7. A numerical target. Any model of how the text was made must now hit about {{v_gram_voy_med}}, 0.04 and 0.01 bits per word on grammar, repeat and vary, along with the line starts and the sheet gradient.
  8. Everything reproducible from pinned public sources, with the numbers in this page computed by the code beside it.

What it takes away

  1. From the popular account: that structure implies meaning; that the book is known to be bound in a recoverable wrong order; the fourteen calves; the villa at Frascati.
  2. From the case for meaningless text: the claim that the copy-and-modify generator matches the manuscript. It matches in kind and overshoots in amount.
  3. From the case for a cipher: any design that works word by word. And the comfort that Naibbe matches the text. It matches its look and none of its local habits.
  4. From our own first report: three readings withdrawn or weakened, listed in the table above.

What it does not do

  • It reads nothing. It names no language, place or maker. Nothing here should be cited as a solution.
  • It has not been reviewed by specialists. It was produced in a single working session by an AI system under one person's direction, with a handful of controls in each class, one cipher and one family of generator. The weights are judgement.
  • It joins a crowded field of machine-assisted analyses. The difference we would claim is method: controls, replication, null tests, and withdrawn claims left on the record.

Where that leaves the larger effort. The question "what is this book?" has come apart into two. How it was made is now a question with accumulating answers: materials, hands, sheets, order of work. What it says may have no answer, and for the first time the two leading explanations have been put on one scale with the manuscript between them. The useful next result is no longer a reading. It is a model of the making, of either kind, that lands on the manuscript's numbers, or a physical finding that the text analysis could not have predicted.

How to validate a tableau

The field has plenty of ideas and no agreed way for one of them to lose. These steps make each tableau a claim that the evidence can score. They are in order, because the later ones depend on the earlier.

  1. Write each tableau as a process you can run

    A scenario becomes testable when it is a procedure that takes inputs (a source text or none, a set of rules, five scribes with their habits, sheets written in page order) and emits pages. The copy-and-modify generator and the Naibbe cipher are the first two such simulators. The meaning-lost tableau needs one. A tableau that cannot be simulated cannot be scored.

  2. Fix the measurements before looking

    Agree on a panel of fingerprints in advance: the six rows of the table above, word structure, the sheet gradient, section and scribe effects. Set aside a share of the sheets that nobody examines until the end. Without this, every analyst tunes until something appears, and the text is small enough ({{n_tokens}} words) to be overfitted by hand.

  3. Calibrate every measurement on texts of known origin

    Before a statistic is trusted on the manuscript it must separate known cases: real languages in period subjects, period ciphers over real text, generated text, and gibberish written by people. Add the classes still missing, above all lists, recipe collections, calendars and tables, and transcriptions that keep original line breaks. Require the result to hold across transliterations.

  4. Score the tableaus by prediction

    Fit each simulator on most of the sheets and ask how well it predicts the words on the held-out ones, in bits per word. Compare a copy model (the next word is a variant of one nearby), a cipher over a language model, and mixtures. This gives a likelihood ratio between tableaus in place of an argument, and it shows how much of the text each one leaves unexplained.

  5. Control for who, what and when

    Scribe, subject and order of writing are tangled together. Compare like with like, as in the same-scribe baseline above. For any result found by searching, such as a best page order or a best key, run the same search on scrambled data to learn what it finds by chance.

  6. Check text against evidence the text never touched

    The strongest validation is agreement between independent kinds of evidence. Physical: the water stain, paint offsets, sewing holes, which sheets were cut from the same skin, which were written with the same batch of ink. Visual: drawings matched to dated traditions by people who have not seen the text results. If the sheet order from stains agrees with the order from text similarity, both gain. If rare words follow a plant from the herbal pages to the pharmacy pages once scribe and order are controlled, the text refers to something.

  7. Hold every claimed reading to one gate

    See the box below. The same gate applies to a scholar's proposal and to a language model's.

  8. Qualify methods on a blind challenge set

    Build a public set of documents made by known processes, some enciphered, some generated, some real languages in invented scripts, all in the same transliteration style, with the answers sealed. A method earns the right to pronounce on the manuscript by classifying these correctly first.

The gate for a claimed decipherment

  1. The mapping from script to language is written down in full and can be applied by someone else without judgement calls.
  2. It has fewer free choices than the text has information. Count the choices; a key with thousands of entries for {{n_types}} word forms explains nothing.
  3. It was fixed before the held-out sheets were opened, and it yields grammatical text there.
  4. Applied to scrambled manuscript text and to generated text, it fails. A method that "reads" gibberish reads nothing.
  5. Run forward on real period text, it reproduces the fingerprints: predictable glyphs, echoing neighbours, line edges, two dialects.
  6. Labels come out as words that suit their pictures more often than labels shuffled between pictures do.
  7. Two people using the key independently produce the same reading of the same page.
